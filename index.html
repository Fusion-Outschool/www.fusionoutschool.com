How We Learn

One Curriculum. Every Subject.

Each unit of study is built around a central theme drawn from our scripture sequence. Every required academic subject is taught through that theme. Students are not switching contexts six times a day. They are going deeper into one coherent body of knowledge from multiple angles simultaneously.


A current unit, for example, moves through Exodus and Numbers. Science covers the chemistry of the Nile, fluid dynamics, hydrology, and the physics of wilderness survival. History covers Egyptian civilization and the archaeology of the ancient Near East. US History follows the Exodus narrative forward through American history — the Puritans, the abolitionists, Frederick Douglass, Harriet Tubman, and Martin Luther King's use of Moses as a political and spiritual framework. ELA is anchored in close reading of the scriptural text itself alongside high-caliber literature. Current events draw live connections to the Nile water dispute between Ethiopia, Egypt, and Sudan — a conflict happening right now involving the same river your student is studying.

This is not thematic decoration.
It is a fundamentally different way of organizing knowledge.

Scripture is NOT Supplementary

It is the organizing principle.

Each day begins with music. Students sing alongside the Tabernacle Choir at Temple Square or with the LDS Youth album — not as performance, not as warm-up, but as an act of invitation. We have found, consistently, that music opens something in a room that nothing else does. It quiets the noise a student carries in from the morning. It orients the heart before the mind begins its work. It invites the Spirit into the study — and in a classroom where revelation is taken seriously as a source of knowledge, that matters as much as any textbook.

From there, the study deepens. Students open their scriptures and go to work — annotating, cross-referencing, tracing a word backward through the text, finding where this passage has appeared before in what they have already read. This is not devotional reading. It is an academic engagement with a sacred text meant to support the family's study of Come Follow Me. Students mark what surprises them, what they did not expect, where the power in the passage lives and where it comes from. The questions that emerge from this opening study, drive everything that follows.

This is the part of the day we protect most fiercely. Everything else can flex. This does not.


But we don't leave them there. Scripture is the academic spine of every unit. Every required subject is taught through and from the scriptural text. Theology does not compete with academics here.


It is the organizing principle.

Literature is the Primary Academic Text

We are not building students who have learned things. We are building students who have learned how to learn — and who have discovered, through the words themselves, that learning is an act of reverence.

We don't assign books for pleasure. We assign them because a great book does something no textbook can — it puts a student inside an experience and refuses to let them observe from a distance.


Students read a minimum of 20 outstanding works of literature per year, studied as primary academic texts. They annotate, analyze, discuss, deliberate, make connections, and challenge the ideas of each work. Every book is chosen deliberately — for its literary weight, its capacity to carry the unit's intellectual and moral load, and its ability to open genuine inquiry.


But reading is only the beginning of what happens here.


Our ELLA — English Language and Literary Arts — program is built on a practice we call Word Genealogy. It begins with a single word drawn from the literature students are reading. We trace that word backward through Latin, Greek, and Hebrew to its oldest root meaning — and then we search for that same root living inside our scripture study.


When a child discovers that a word in the novel they are holding shares its ancient root with a word God uses in Leviticus to command how Israel treats the vulnerable, something happens that no worksheet can produce. The child sees that language is not arbitrary. Words carry their histories every time they are spoken or written. Those histories connect across centuries, across languages, across texts — to reveal a single continuous truth.


This is not vocabulary instruction. It is not literary analysis. It is not Bible study — though it draws on all three. It is the pursuit of wisdom: the recognition that all true knowledge is connected, and that a child who learns to trace those connections becomes a reader, a thinker, and a person of faith who cannot be satisfied with surfaces.


Students work inside their texts — writing in margins, diagramming sentences, debating which word in a chapter is doing the most work, defending claims with evidence from two texts written thousands of years apart.


We are not building students who have learned things. We are building students who have learned how to learn — and who have discovered, through the words themselves, that learning is an act of reverence.

Rigorous By Design

We don't fit students to a curriculum. We find the curriculum worthy of the student.

Academic resources across all disciplines are chosen by one standard: rigor, depth, and quality — matched to the individual student.


For Mathematics, that might mean:

  • Art of Problem Solving — for a student ready for deep theoretical challenge.
  • The Good and The Beautiful — for a student who thrives with a curriculum that weaves beauty, character, and academic content together.
  • Saxon Math — for a student who needs a structured, sequential foundation.
  • Or something designed entirely around a student's passion. A student who loves history doesn't leave that love at the door when math begins. That love becomes the math — historical events carefully chosen to teach real mathematical concepts through real-world application. Rote memorization becomes project-based investigation.

No two students learn at exactly the same pace or in exactly the same way. Curriculum is selected for each child individually — chosen to honor where they are and to challenge them toward what they have not yet reached.


We don't fit students to a curriculum. We find the curriculum worthy of the student.

Where Thinking Becomes Accountable

We are not preparing students to perform. We are preparing them to stand behind what they know.

At most schools, writing is something students do to show they completed the work. Here, writing is the work.


Every subject produces writing. Students write from literature, from scripture, from scientific investigation, from historical research. They produce analytical essays, compare-and-contrast arguments, research reports, and sustained journal entries that accumulate across an entire unit. By the time a formal essay is assigned, a student has weeks of their own thinking to draw from. The writing almost produces itself — because the inquiry that preceded it was genuine.


Writing culminates in the Colloquium — a formal academic presentation in which students present their researched position to their peers, without notes, and then defend it under questioning. This is not a book report. A student stands before their intellectual community, states what they believe and why, and then answers for it. Peers question. The teacher asks the question nobody asked. The student defends — or revises their position publicly — and posts their final claim on the wall.


Where a second grader watches an eighth grader defend a claim under questioning and understands, without being told, what it looks like to think seriously.


We are not preparing students to perform. We are preparing them to stand behind what they know.

The R&R Method — Reason and Revelation

Your child will not be told what to think here. They will be taught how to pursue truth — and trusted to find it.

Every day begins with a question. Formed by the students from their encounter with the material the day before, written on the board exactly as they said it, and pursued from there. A student whose question is driving the day is a fundamentally different learner than one who is answering someone else's.


The R&R Method — Reason and Revelation is the eight-step inquiry cycle that governs how every student here encounters new knowledge:

  • Observe — encounter the material first. Scripture, a text, a phenomenon. Receive it before analyzing it.
  • Question — form your own question. It goes on the board. It drives what follows.
  • Search — pursue the question through primary sources. The actual text. The actual data. Not a summary — the thing itself.
  • Assert — take a position. A student who has done the searching earns the right to say I think.
  • Exercise — experiment and engineer. Test what you believe is true. Build it, run it, measure it. Find out whether your claim holds under real conditions.
  • Gather — collect evidence. Sit with contradiction. Revise.
  • Record — write what you found in your own words. This is how we know understanding has happened.
  • Create — make something from what you learned. Present it. Defend it.

The teacher's role in this cycle is not to deliver answers. It is to open doors — offering a perspective the student hasn't considered, a source they haven't found, a question that pushes their thinking further than it would go alone. The teacher holds the map but does not show it. They challenge, redirect, and raise the ceiling — one student at a time.


Every session opens with personal prayer. They ask the Lord to help them know, for help in understanding and applying what they found.


Gratitude runs around the outside of everything, but as the acknowledgment of where truth comes from. Amongst their written records is kept a gratitude record - tender reminders of God's mercies and love.


Your child will not be told what to think here. They will be taught how to pursue truth — and trusted to find it.

Truth. Shared.

Every unit of study at this school produces something that exists beyond the classroom — not a grade, but something real that goes into the world.

The R&R Method closes with Create. That word is chosen carefully. Students don't produce a demonstration of learning for a teacher to evaluate and file away. They make something — something that exists beyond the assessment, beyond the unit, beyond the classroom walls.


This is a permanent feature of how this school works, not a special project or a seasonal add-on. Every unit of study is designed with a question that extends outward: What does the world need from what we just learned?


Sometimes the answer is a physical object — built, painted, carried into a public space, seen by people who weren't in the room when the learning happened. Sometimes it is a presentation made to a real audience with real stakes. Sometimes it is a contribution to an ongoing need in the community or the world — funded, organized, and advocated for by students who understand the problem deeply enough to explain it to anyone.


The current unit is studying water — its science, its history, its theology, its scarcity, its politics. Students are building a mosaic well, tile by tile, as the learning accumulates. Each tile is a record of something understood. The well travels. It is seen. And at the end of the unit, a real borehole well is funded and built in Sudan. Two wells exist when the unit ends. One holds the learning. One holds the water.


But the well is not the point. The point is the posture — the conviction that a student who has genuinely learned something has also acquired a responsibility. To understand water scarcity is to be obligated to the 2.2 billion people who live inside it. To understand the Exodus is to be obligated to everyone still walking through a wilderness. To understand how to read and write and think is to be obligated to use those tools for something beyond yourself.


We are not preparing students to accumulate knowledge. We are preparing them to steward it.


What students learn here does not stay here. It goes into the world — and it changes something real.

What is an outSchool?

An outSchool is not a traditional private school. It is not a homeschool co-op in the conventional sense either. It is something more intentional than both.


The "out" is deliberate. We are outside the standardized system — outside the pacing guides, the standardized tests, the grade-level boxes, the subject silos that keep math from talking to history and scripture from talking to science. We operate outside those constraints because we believe they limit what children are capable of becoming.


Legally, ZION outSchool is registered with the Arkansas Department of Education as an EFA-participating microschool. That means we are a recognized private learning institution — not a hobby, not an informal arrangement, not a co-op that dissolves when the founding families move on. We are a school with a designed curriculum, trained teachers, a defined methodology, and accountability to the families we serve.


Practically, what that means for your family is this: your child comes to a real classroom, with a real teacher, doing real academic work — four days a week. The fifth day belongs to the home, because we believe the home is the first classroom and that day is how we honor that.


Under Arkansas law, parents who enroll their child here retain legal responsibility for their student's education. We are your educational partner — rigorous, accountable, and deeply committed — but the authority over your child's learning remains where it belongs: with you.


An outSchool is what happens when you take education seriously enough to build something new.

An eighth grader who has to explain a concept to a second grader discovers quickly whether they actually understand it.

Faith & Membership

Our curriculum is rooted in the LDS standard works, including the Book of Mormon and the Come Follow Me program. Membership in the LDS church is not required for enrollment. What scriptures do you teach from?

We teach from the LDS standard works — the four volumes of scripture recognized by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints:

  • The Bible (King James Version)
  • The Book of Mormon
  • The Doctrine and Covenants
  • The Pearl of Great Price
  • The words of modern day Prophets and Apostles.

Scripture serves as the organizing spine of each unit of study — not as a devotional addition, but as the primary academic text from which science, history, literature, and language arts all radiate outward.

What is Come Follow Me?

Come Follow Me is a scripture study curriculum developed by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. It moves through the standard works on a rotating annual cycle, providing structured study questions, context, and commentary designed for both individual and family use.

At ZION outSchool, Come Follow Me provides the scriptural sequence that anchors each unit of study. Rather than using it as a devotional program, we build our entire academic curriculum around it — treating each scripture block as the lens through which science, history, literature, and language arts are studied that unit.

You do not need to be familiar with Come Follow Me before enrolling. Students encounter it as part of their academic work here.

How is the LDS faith different from other Christian denominations?

This is a question worth answering honestly, because families of other Christian backgrounds deserve to know what they are enrolling their child into.

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints holds Jesus Christ as its center and Savior — that is the shared foundation with other Christian traditions. Several doctrinal distinctives set it apart:

  • LDS theology holds that God the Father, Jesus Christ, and the Holy Ghost are three distinct beings — not one unified Trinity as defined by the Nicene Creed.
  • The Church believes in continuing revelation — that God speaks through living prophets today, as He did in ancient scripture.
  • The Book of Mormon is accepted as a second witness of Jesus Christ alongside the Bible, not a replacement of it.
  • LDS doctrine teaches that humans are literal spirit children of God with divine potential — a view of human nature that shapes how we think about children and learning.

These beliefs are not hidden or softened here. They are woven into the curriculum because they are the foundation of how we understand truth, knowledge, and the purpose of education. Families who enroll their children here should understand and be at peace with that foundation — even if they do not share every point of doctrine.

If you have questions about LDS beliefs before enrolling, we are happy to talk through them directly. Reach out here.

Do you issue transcripts?

Yes. We maintain detailed records of each student's coursework, reading, writing, and demonstrated progress across every unit of study. We provide families with comprehensive documentation of their student's academic work upon request.

Because we are not currently accredited, our transcripts carry the weight of a homeschool record under Arkansas law — which is more substantial than it may sound. Homeschool graduates are actively recruited by colleges and universities, and admissions officers are experienced in evaluating non-traditional academic records. What matters is the quality and depth of the work — and we document that thoroughly.

Under Arkansas law, the homeschool diploma itself is parent-issued. We support families through that process and provide all necessary documentation. View Arkansas homeschool diploma requirements.

What happens if we return to public school?

For students in grades 2–8, the transition back to public school is typically straightforward. Arkansas public schools place transfer students by age and conduct their own assessment of academic readiness — they do not rely on private school transcripts or accreditation status to determine placement.

In practice, students coming from ZION outSchool tend to test above grade level. The depth of analytical reading, writing, and mathematical reasoning they build here consistently places them ahead of where a public school would expect them to be.

We provide families with thorough documentation of all coursework completed, which can accompany a student to any new school and give receiving teachers a clear picture of what the student knows and can do.

If this is a concern for your family, bring it to the conversation when you reach out. We would rather address it honestly than have it be a surprise later.

Testing — what is required and what is our approach?

We do not teach to standardized tests. We do not administer them in school. We do not design curriculum around them.

We teach students to read deeply, write analytically, reason from evidence, and defend positions under questioning. Those skills transfer directly to standardized assessments — not because we prepared students for the test, but because we prepared students to think. The test measures a fraction of what they are capable of, and they tend to exceed it.

EFA testing requirement: Students receiving EFA funds are required to take a nationally norm-referenced standardized test each year. This is an EFA program requirement — not ours. We support families in meeting this requirement and provide guidance on approved tests and scheduling.

What is the Arkansas Education Freedom Account (EFA)?

The Arkansas Education Freedom Account (EFA) is a state-funded program that gives every eligible K–12 student in Arkansas up to $7,208 per year for approved educational expenses — including private school tuition, unit fees, the annual curriculum fee, uniforms, supplies, tutoring, and technology.

Funds are deposited quarterly into a ClassWallet account that you manage directly — typically in August, October, February, and April. You direct those funds to approved schools and vendors through the platform.

As of the 2025–2026 school year, the EFA is universal. Every Arkansas K–12 student is eligible regardless of income, zip code, or prior school enrollment.

$7,208 reflects the expected 2026–2027 amount, subject to final confirmation by the Arkansas Department of Education.

Is this an EFA-participating school?

Yes. We are a confirmed EFA-participating private school. Once your application is approved, you link your student to us inside ClassWallet and we invoice you directly through the platform each quarter.

Our total annual enrollment cost is $6,710 — tuition, all unit fees, and the annual curriculum fee included. For most families, this leaves $498 in remaining EFA funds for your student's required uniform and other approved expenses.

For qualifying families, your out-of-pocket cost is zero.

How do I apply for the EFA?

New families apply through the FACTS portal at dese.link/efa. The application takes approximately 15–20 minutes per child. Have these ready:

  • Student's birth certificate
  • Proof of Arkansas residency (driver's license, utility bill, lease agreement, or similar)

Applications are reviewed on a rolling basis and typically take up to 10 days. The application and renewal window for the 2026–2027 school year is March 9 – June 1.

Visit the Arkansas Department of Education for full program details →

Already have an EFA? You must renew every year.

⚠️ EFA funding is not automatically continued from one year to the next. If you do not renew, your funding lapses for the entire school year — with no exceptions.

Returning families complete a renewal survey inside ClassWallet — faster than the original application, no documents to re-upload. Renewal window: March 9 – June 1.

Log in at dese.link/efa and complete your renewal before June 1.

What can I use remaining EFA funds for?

After tuition, unit fees, and the annual curriculum fee, most families have approximately $498 in remaining EFA funds. Those funds can be used for:

  • Required uniform — Scrubs, any color. We recommend RMF Scrubs — see the Tuition section for direct links. EFA-eligible. Submit for reimbursement.
  • Supplemental supplies
  • Testing fees — Including standardized testing required for EFA renewal
  • Technology — Devices and educational subscriptions (excludes TVs, video games, and cell phones)
  • Tutoring — From an approved EFA service provider

Do not leave funds unspent at year end. Unused funds may roll over up to a state-set limit — beyond that they return to the state.

Is there additional scholarship funding available beyond the EFA?

Yes. The Philanthropic Investment in Arkansas Kids (PIAK) scholarship is a separate, privately-funded program for income-qualifying Arkansas families. It is administered through The Reform Alliance and can be used to pay tuition at participating private schools.

Here is how PIAK relates to the EFA — and why it matters for your family:

Most families: choose one or the other.

The EFA is available to every Arkansas student regardless of income. PIAK is available only to families whose household income is at or below 200% of the federal poverty line — approximately $63,400 for a family of four. If your family qualifies for both, you generally must choose one. Most income-qualifying families will find the EFA sufficient to cover tuition at ZION outSchool.

Families with a documented disability: can stack both.

If your child has a qualifying disability and your household income qualifies, you may receive both the EFA and the PIAK scholarship simultaneously. That is potentially $7,208 in EFA funds plus up to $6,500 in PIAK funds — combined funding that can cover tuition and significant additional educational expenses. This is one of the most underused opportunities available to Arkansas families.

PIAK as a backup if EFA funding runs out.

The EFA is funded by the state legislature and is not guaranteed for every applicant — if the allocation runs out, some families may be waitlisted. If your family is income-qualified and you are waiting on EFA approval or have missed the EFA window, PIAK may be able to cover your tuition in the meantime. There is no fixed PIAK deadline, but funding is capped annually and awards stop when the cap is reached. Apply early.

To qualify for PIAK:

  • Household income at or below 200% of the federal poverty line — approximately $63,400 for a family of four
  • Student must have attended public school at some point during the 2024–2025 school year
  • Exceptions apply for students with qualifying disabilities, students enrolling in kindergarten for the first time, and families relocating to Arkansas from out of state

PIAK award amounts:

  • Grades K–8: up to $6,500
  • Grades 9–12: up to $7,300
  • Award will not exceed the actual cost of tuition and fees

What counts as a qualifying disability?

Qualifying disabilities are defined by the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). The 13 recognized categories are:

  • Autism Spectrum Disorder
  • Specific Learning Disability — includes dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, auditory processing disorder, and nonverbal learning disability
  • Speech or Language Impairment — includes stuttering, impaired articulation, and language disorders
  • Other Health Impairment — includes ADHD and conditions that limit a child's strength, energy, or alertness
  • Emotional Disturbance
  • Intellectual Disability
  • Hearing Impairment (including Deafness)
  • Visual Impairment (including Blindness)
  • Orthopedic Impairment
  • Traumatic Brain Injury
  • Deaf-Blindness
  • Multiple Disabilities
  • Developmental Delay

Having a diagnosis alone is not sufficient. The disability must be formally documented through one of the following: district testing within the last three years identifying an IDEA disability, a current Individualized Education Program (IEP), or a signed medical diagnosis form from a licensed professional.

⚠️ No fixed deadline — but apply early. PIAK scholarships are funded through private donations with a state-set annual cap. Funding runs out when the cap is reached. Applications for the 2026–2027 school year are currently open. Families with qualifying disabilities especially should not wait — stacking EFA and PIAK requires both applications to be approved before the school year begins.
